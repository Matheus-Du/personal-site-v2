Stanisaw Lem's Solaris is a science-fiction novel with a scale appropriate for the genre despite taking place almost entirely in a space station not much larger than an apartment block. The planet Solaris is the home of an alien species with a population of 1. However, that single resident is a viscous liquid weighing 700 billion tons and covering the planet's surface. After trying a new experiment on the creature, the residents of the station on Solaris find themselves subjects of the creature's own experiment, yielding a psychological thriller that takes place in the backdrop of a scientific attempt to understand the incomprehensible.

	Lem does an excellent job of creating fictional scientific reports, studies, and journals about the creature, creating an atmosphere of awe and confusion using scientific terms and phenomena. We leave the novel feeling as if we just undertook a rigourous introductory study of a real alien creature. Describing the nature of fictional concepts like mimoids and symmetriads using scientific terminology both helps set the atmosphere of the story and tricks us into believing these phenomena to be real by way of our conflation with scientific jargon and truthfulness.

	The spoke of the novel is the folly of attempting to understand or communicate with an alien creature as incomprehensible as Solaris. What is to us psychological horror and torment is certainly not that to the creature, who views us as ants at best and electrons at worst. We're reminded of our own insignificance on a cosmological scale, not just in size but in our capacity to even comprehend phenomena, much less understand them. The novel covers an absolutely massive amount of scientific classification, genius, and theorizing and leaves us with no answers or insights into how or why the events of the novel took place. We're left only with a deeper understanding of our own incapacity to delve into even the more minor mysteries of the Universe.
